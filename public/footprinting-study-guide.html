Use this in a study session

Spend 15 minutes recalling service roles and setting one goal, 60 minutes on a bounded lab question, and 15 minutes reviewing evidence. Split this large module across your 10-hour study week as needed. In your weekly note, record goals, attempts, what you learned, where you got stuck and progress. Mark a lesson complete only when you can explain the result and its limits.

01. Enumeration Principles

Footprinting builds a supported picture of an environment: what exists, what each service exposes, and which questions remain. Work in short loops: observe, form a hypothesis, choose a narrow check, record the result, then revise. A banner is a clue, not proof of the exact software or its security. A failed probe may reflect filtering, privileges or an incorrect protocol assumption.

Keep observed facts separate from interpretations. Reading third-party public records differs from sending requests to a target; visiting its website or querying its authoritative DNS still interacts with infrastructure. Agree on scope, allowed techniques and rate limits before active checks. The examples here use assigned lab systems and synthetic identifiers. 

Observation: TCP 445 responds on ASSIGNED_LAB_IP
Hypothesis: SMB service; permissions unknown
Next check: identify protocol and list shares in the allowed identity context
Evidence: timestamp, command, output, identity and target
Result / uncertainty: ...

Checkpoint: Before starting, write down the authorized targets and one question you want each probe to answer.

02. Enumeration Methodology

HTB organizes enumeration into six layers: Internet Presence, Gateway, Accessible Services, Processes, Privileges and OS Setup. Treat this as a course model for organizing questions, rather than a universal network standard or a rigid order. Infrastructure clues lead to hosts; service behavior suggests processes; a verified identity reveals a different view of permissions and operating-system configuration.

Revisit earlier layers when evidence changes. A hostname found in a permitted share might explain a certificate name or identify a service to verify, but discovering that hostname does not add it to scope. Preserve the relationship and request scope clarification when needed. 

Internet presence → names, addresses, public services
Gateway → routing and filtering observations
Accessible services → protocol, version clues, entry requirements
Processes → application behavior and dependencies
Privileges → identity and permitted operations
OS setup → configuration and trust relationships

Checkpoint: Choose a layer for each finding. Record the next unanswered question, not just another tool to run.

03. Domain Information

Build a small inventory of names and their sources. A and AAAA records map names to addresses; MX identifies mail routing; NS and SOA describe DNS authority; TXT may disclose verification or mail-policy metadata. Query explicit record types rather than assuming an ANY query returns everything. State which resolver answered and whether a result is authoritative or cached.

Certificate-transparency records and historical DNS can suggest related names. Dates matter: a former certificate name, shared address or third-party provider does not establish current ownership or authorization. Verify a candidate against the agreed scope before contacting it. Record negative results too; absence from one source is not proof that a service does not exist. 

DOMAIN=lab.example
DNS_SERVER=ASSIGNED_DNS_IP
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" A
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" AAAA
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" MX
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" NS
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" SOA
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" TXT

Checkpoint: Create a name → address → evidence-source table. Mark historical names and unresolved ownership questions.

04. Cloud Resources

Cloud hostnames can suggest S3, Azure Blob Storage, Google Cloud Storage or hosted application services. First determine the provider, the exact resource name and whether the resource is explicitly in scope. Provider ownership of an address is different from customer ownership of a resource. Public application assets are often intentional.

Distinguish permission to list a container from permission to read a known object, and both from permission to write. A listing denial does not establish object privacy. For a known lab bucket, inspect only the permitted listing or a small designated test object; avoid guessing names, bulk mirroring or uploading test files. Keep tokens, signed URLs and recovered secrets out of public notes. 

# Only a known, explicitly authorized lab bucket
BUCKET=ASSIGNED_LAB_BUCKET
aws s3 ls "s3://$BUCKET/" --no-sign-request

Checkpoint: Capture the provider, resource identifier, tested operation and observed permission separately.

05. Staff

Organization-owned job postings, technical articles and public repositories can reveal vocabulary, product families and deployment practices. Use those clues to prioritize verification on authorized systems. A job advert mentioning a database does not prove the current production version or that every subsidiary uses it.

Prefer technical evidence over personal profiling. Record the publication date and confidence, and avoid copying personal contact information, contacting employees or testing credentials found in public sources. If a potential secret appears, use the agreed reporting process rather than validating it against a real service. The goal is a defensible technology hypothesis, not a list of people. 

Source URL / publication date: ...
Technical clue: approved posting mentions a managed database
Hypothesis: verify database protocol on an in-scope lab service
Confidence / age / alternative explanations: ...
Validation evidence: ...

Checkpoint: Write one technology hypothesis and a specific authorized observation that would confirm or weaken it.

06. FTP

FTP normally uses TCP 21 for its control connection and a separate data connection. Passive data ports are negotiated; active-mode server data connections commonly originate from TCP 20. A successful login does not guarantee the data channel works through a firewall. Check the connection mode before interpreting a stalled listing.

FTPS is not SFTP: FTPS adds TLS to FTP, while SFTP runs over SSH. TFTP is another protocol, commonly using UDP 69, without FTP-style login. Test anonymous access only when allowed, then compare it with a supplied account. Read a designated lab file rather than recursively downloading everything. The named Nmap scripts below check service information and anonymous listing, so confirm their behavior with script help first. 

TARGET=ASSIGNED_LAB_IP
nmap --script-help ftp-syst,ftp-anon
nmap -sV -p21 --script ftp-syst,ftp-anon "$TARGET"
$ ftp -p ASSIGNED_LAB_IP
Name: SUPPLIED_LAB_USER
Password: [enter at prompt]
ftp> status
ftp> pwd
ftp> ls
ftp> get KNOWN_LAB_FILE
ftp> bye

Checkpoint: Explain whether each result came from authentication, directory permissions or a data-channel problem.

07. SMB

SMB commonly uses TCP 445, with older NetBIOS transport on TCP 139. Keep anonymous, guest and authenticated observations separate. Some servers map an attempted identity to guest, so record what the server actually accepted. Modern SMB does not imply SMB1 support.

A visible share does not prove access to its contents or permission to write. Share configuration and underlying filesystem permissions both influence the result. Use a supplied account interactively so its password is not embedded in the command. Inspect known directories within scope; do not upload a permission-test file unless the exercise explicitly calls for writes. A null-session RPC response is evidence about that request, not a general authorization bypass. 

smbclient -N -L "//$TARGET"
# Optional null-session information check, only in the allowed lab
rpcclient -N -U '' "$TARGET" -c 'srvinfo'
SHARE=VERIFIED_LAB_SHARE
LAB_USER=SUPPLIED_LAB_USER
smbclient "//$TARGET/$SHARE" -U "$LAB_USER"
# Enter password at the prompt; use pwd, ls and exit interactively

Checkpoint: Build a matrix of identity, visible share, readable path and tested operation. Leave untested writes marked unknown.

08. NFS

NFS commonly uses port 2049. NFSv3 often also depends on rpcbind on port 111 and auxiliary RPC services; NFSv4 can operate without those discovery services. Consequently, an empty showmount result does not rule out a v4-only export. NFSv4 does not automatically require Kerberos: inspect the security flavor rather than inferring it from the version.

With AUTH_SYS, numeric user and group IDs matter. root_squash maps requests from UID 0 to an anonymous identity; it does not make all other file access safe. A client-side read-only mount reduces accidental writes but does not prove the export is read-only on the server. Mount only the verified export and unmount it when finished. 

rpcinfo -p "$TARGET"
showmount -e "$TARGET"
mkdir -p mnt/nfs
# Replace VERIFIED_EXPORT with the exact authorized export path
sudo mount -t nfs -o ro,nosuid,nodev "$TARGET:/VERIFIED_EXPORT" mnt/nfs
ls -ln mnt/nfs
sudo umount mnt/nfs

Checkpoint: Note the NFS version, export path, security flavor and numeric ownership, including any unresolved assumptions.

09. DNS

DNS uses UDP and TCP 53. Distinguish authoritative answers, recursive resolution and cached data. Query a known server for a known zone. A version.bind response may expose a configured string, but it is not reliable proof of the implementation or patch level.

A zone transfer can reveal many records at once, but attempt it only for an explicitly authorized lab zone and nameserver. AXFR normally uses TCP. A refused transfer means this request was denied; it does not mean the zone has no records or that all DNS configuration is sound. Compare discovered records with scope before probing any resulting addresses. Keep broad dictionary enumeration out of the initial baseline. 

dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" SOA
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" NS
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" version.bind CH TXT
# Explicitly authorized zone-transfer test only
dig @"$DNS_SERVER" "$DOMAIN" AXFR

Checkpoint: Explain which server supplied each answer and whether the transfer result changes your next question.

10. SMTP

SMTP sends and relays mail. TCP 25 commonly handles relay, 587 submission with STARTTLS, and 465 submission with implicit TLS. EHLO capabilities describe advertised features; they do not by themselves establish relay permission, a valid account or successful authentication. STARTTLS upgrades a connection and is separate from login.

Begin with greeting and capability evidence. Do not send test mail, enumerate recipients or attempt relay without explicit exercise authorization. Responses to account checks can be obscured by catch-all behavior and policy. For TLS checks, use the verified mail hostname and a trusted CA; a matching certificate and a successful TLS handshake still do not prove mailbox access. 

$ nc ASSIGNED_LAB_IP 25
EHLO lab.example
QUIT
# Record the greeting and capabilities; no message is submitted
MAIL_HOST=VERIFIED_LAB_MAIL_HOST
LAB_CA=/path/to/supplied-lab-ca.pem
openssl s_client -starttls smtp -connect "$TARGET:587" \
  -servername "$MAIL_HOST" -verify_return_error \
  -verify_hostname "$MAIL_HOST" -CAfile "$LAB_CA"
# After TLS negotiation: EHLO lab.example, then QUIT

Checkpoint: Distinguish a transport finding, an authentication finding and a relay-policy finding in your notes.

11. IMAP / POP3

IMAP synchronizes mailbox state; POP3 provides message retrieval with deletion behavior controlled by client commands and server policy. Neither protocol is the mail-submission service. IMAP commonly uses TCP 143 with STARTTLS or 993 with implicit TLS; POP3 uses 110 with STARTTLS or 995 with implicit TLS.

Capabilities can be observed before login where supported. A successful login or mailbox listing is different from permission to read a particular message. Use only supplied accounts and designated lab messages, and avoid destructive mailbox commands. With curl, omitting the password from --user requests it interactively. The example keeps hostname verification intact while connecting to the assigned address. 

curl --cacert "$LAB_CA" --resolve "$MAIL_HOST:993:$TARGET" \
  --user "$LAB_USER" "imaps://$MAIL_HOST/"
# Inside a verified TLS IMAP session
a1 CAPABILITY
a2 LOGOUT

# Inside a verified TLS POP3 session
CAPA
QUIT

Checkpoint: Record the transport, certificate name, accepted identity and exact mailbox operation; do not publish message contents.

12. SNMP

SNMP agents commonly receive queries on UDP 161, while traps commonly go to UDP 162. OIDs identify objects in a management information tree. Query a small known subtree before considering a wider walk: device metadata can be sensitive even when it does not contain credentials.

SNMPv1 and v2c community strings travel without protocol encryption. SNMPv3 supports several security levels; only choosing an authenticated, private mode provides both authentication and encryption. Do not infer confidentiality from “v3” alone. Use a supplied lab community rather than guessing strings. CLI arguments can appear in process listings and logs, so use only disposable lab values in these examples. Avoid SET operations, which can change device state. 

LAB_COMMUNITY=SUPPLIED_DISPOSABLE_LAB_COMMUNITY
snmpget -v2c -c "$LAB_COMMUNITY" "$TARGET" 1.3.6.1.2.1.1.1.0
# Bounded system subtree, rather than walking the entire device
snmpwalk -v2c -c "$LAB_COMMUNITY" "$TARGET" 1.3.6.1.2.1.1

Checkpoint: Associate each useful OID with its meaning and distinguish the agent description from independently verified facts.

13. MySQL

MySQL commonly listens on TCP 3306. A greeting may identify a product family, but successful login requires an allowed account and host context. The identity requested by a client can differ from the effective account selected by the server. TLS defaults and verification settings vary by client version; inspect the installed client and lab connection requirements rather than disabling encryption to make a connection work.

Start with version, identity, visible schemas and grants. A schema name is metadata, not permission to read every table. Query only the designated dataset when required, and avoid account tables, dumps and configuration changes. The interactive -p option prompts for a password instead of placing it on the command line. 

mysql --host="$TARGET" --port=3306 --user="$LAB_USER" -p
SELECT VERSION(), CURRENT_USER(), USER();
SHOW DATABASES;
SHOW GRANTS;
-- In an explicitly permitted schema:
USE VERIFIED_LAB_SCHEMA;
SHOW TABLES;

Checkpoint: Compare requested identity, effective account and grants. Explain why visible schema metadata is not a data-access finding.

14. MSSQL

Microsoft SQL Server often uses TCP 1433 for TDS, but named instances can listen on other ports. SQL Browser discovery commonly uses UDP 1434 and is not always enabled. A client application such as SSMS is not evidence that a particular server or instance is reachable.

Separate SQL authentication from Windows authentication. Use the connection mode the lab actually supplies and preserve credential prompts. The Impacket command below selects Windows authentication; a supplied SQL login uses the corresponding SQL-authentication connection instead, without that flag. Begin with identity and database metadata. Visibility of a database does not prove administrative rights, operating-system command execution or access to its contents. 

# Replace each synthetic identifier; password is requested interactively
impacket-mssqlclient 'LAB_DOMAIN/LAB_USER@ASSIGNED_LAB_IP' -windows-auth
SELECT @@VERSION;
SELECT SYSTEM_USER, ORIGINAL_LOGIN();
SELECT name FROM sys.databases;

Checkpoint: Write down the instance address, authentication mode, effective login and database visibility. Keep untested privileges unknown.

15. Oracle TNS

An Oracle listener commonly accepts connections on TCP 1521 and directs them to registered services. An open listener is not the same as a usable database session. SID and service name are not interchangeable: obtain the identifier required by the exercise and use the matching connection syntax. The Easy Connect example below uses a service name.

TNS does not automatically imply encrypted transport. Follow the lab’s Oracle Net or TLS configuration instead of assuming port 1521 protects credentials. After an authorized login, inspect your current user, session privileges and owned table metadata. Avoid default-password guessing, SYSDBA bypass attempts and automated tool modes that upload files or perform broad checks. 

nmap -sV -p1521 "$TARGET"
ORACLE_SERVICE=VERIFIED_LAB_SERVICE
sqlplus -L "${LAB_USER}@//${TARGET}:1521/${ORACLE_SERVICE}"
SELECT USER FROM dual;
SELECT privilege FROM session_privs;
SELECT table_name FROM user_tables;

Checkpoint: Explain the listener → service → authenticated session relationship, including what each observation does not establish.

16. IPMI

IPMI exposes out-of-band hardware management through a baseboard management controller, often on UDP 623. A BMC operates independently of the host operating system but still needs appropriate standby power and network access. Treat its management interface as a distinct asset with its own scope and identity.

A version response confirms only the observed management protocol behavior. It does not establish a weak password or vulnerable authentication configuration. Prefer a narrow version check and, with supplied credentials, a read-only controller-information request. Do not reset power, change configuration, capture authentication material or run password attacks as part of this baseline. 

sudo nmap -sU -p623 --script ipmi-version "$TARGET"
# Read-only controller information; -a prompts for the supplied password
ipmitool -I lanplus -H "$TARGET" -U "$LAB_USER" -a mc info

Checkpoint: Keep host-OS evidence and controller evidence separate. Explain why their availability can differ.

17. Linux Remote Management Protocols

SSH commonly uses TCP 22 for remote access. Verify the host-key fingerprint through the lab’s trusted channel before accepting a new host; do not bypass host-key checking to remove a warning. Verbose client output helps separate name resolution, transport negotiation, host identity and account authentication failures.

An rsync daemon commonly uses TCP 873 and rsync:// URLs; rsync over SSH is a different transport. A module listing does not establish permission to read every path or write files. Legacy rexec, rlogin and rsh commonly use TCP 512, 513 and 514, with trust models worth investigating in an assigned legacy lab. UDP 514 is commonly syslog, so include the transport when describing a port. 

ssh -v "$LAB_USER@$TARGET"
# Verify the displayed fingerprint against the supplied trusted value
# List authorized daemon modules without copying their contents
rsync --list-only "rsync://$TARGET/"
# After verifying a permitted module name:
rsync --list-only "rsync://$TARGET/VERIFIED_MODULE/"

Checkpoint: Explain whether a failure occurred before authentication, during authentication or when accessing a requested object.

18. Windows Remote Management Protocols

RDP commonly uses TCP 3389. WinRM commonly uses 5985 for HTTP and 5986 for HTTPS. WMI over DCOM uses RPC endpoint mapping on TCP 135 plus negotiated ports; a CIM session over WSMan is a different transport. Identify the mechanism instead of grouping all Windows management under one open port.

HTTP on 5985 alone does not prove management messages are plaintext: negotiated Kerberos or NTLM message encryption can apply. Verify authentication and transport configuration. For RDP, use the installed FreeRDP executable, confirm certificate identity and enter credentials at its prompt. The PowerShell example uses HTTPS WSMan with a verified hostname and trusted certificate; it reads OS metadata and removes the session afterward. 

nmap -sV -p135,3389,5985,5986 --script rdp-ntlm-info "$TARGET"
# Some installations name this executable xfreerdp3
xfreerdp /v:"$TARGET" /u:"$LAB_USER"
$cred = Get-Credential
$options = New-CimSessionOption -UseSsl
$session = New-CimSession -ComputerName 'VERIFIED_LAB_HOST' -SessionOption $options -Credential $cred
Get-CimInstance -ClassName Win32_OperatingSystem -CimSession $session
Remove-CimSession $session

Checkpoint: Identify which result belongs to RDP, DCOM or WSMan and which identity produced it.

19. Footprinting Lab - Easy

Treat the easy lab as a complete enumeration exercise rather than a race to an answer. Read the current task and use only its assigned target, account and allowed services. Start with the internal DNS context, connect verified names to services, and inspect only files that the task permits. Do not copy the module’s credentials into a public guide or reuse them elsewhere.

Preserve a short evidence chain: the clue, why it justified the next check, the authenticated context and the observed result. If a request fails, compare protocol, hostname and permissions before changing tools. Keep aggressive exploitation out of a footprinting task. The template below deliberately contains no flag or assessment solution. 

Easy lab evidence
Scope / supplied identity: [store secrets privately]
DNS observation: ...
Verified service and permitted path: ...
Command → relevant output → interpretation: ...
Next narrow check / reason: ...
What I learned / what remains uncertain: ...

Checkpoint: Explain your evidence chain aloud before submitting an answer in HTB.

20. Footprinting Lab - Medium

The medium lab adds opportunities to connect findings across services. Read the current scenario carefully: an additional internal system is in scope only when the exercise explicitly includes it. A path, hostname or account hint found on one service is a hypothesis to verify, not permission to follow every relationship.

Keep a service matrix that records transport, authentication context and accessible metadata. Compare a clue with an independent observation before drawing a conclusion. When blocked, return to the exact unanswered question: is the name correct, is the service reachable, did the account authenticate, or was an operation denied? Change one assumption at a time and retain the failed attempt in your private notes. 

Source service / observed clue: ...
Related asset / explicit scope basis: ...
Hypothesis: ...
Smallest verification / identity used: ...
Observed result / alternative explanation: ...
Evidence link and next step: ...

Checkpoint: Find one cross-service relationship and document why it is supported, without publishing credentials or answers.

21. Footprinting Lab - Hard

For the hard lab, separate mail handling, remote management and backup-related services into distinct roles. Build a relationship map from observed names, protocol behavior and authorized metadata. Do not assume that a shared hostname, account name or apparent backup path gives the same access on every service.

Work from your evidence log, not from an ever-growing command list. Time-box an unproductive hypothesis, record why it stalled and return to the service matrix. A useful completion record explains which observations established the result and what remains unverified. Submit assessment answers only in HTB; keep flags, recovered files and secret material out of the public application. 

Hard lab review
Service roles / verified relationships: ...
Strongest evidence / authenticated context: ...
Attempt that failed / corrected assumption: ...
Remaining uncertainty / next authorized check: ...
Progress: [not started / investigating / evidence complete]
Weekly reflection: goal, attempts, learning, blockers, next action

Checkpoint: Finish with a reproducible explanation and one improvement to your next weekly study session.
